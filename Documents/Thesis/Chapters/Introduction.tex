\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}
The manufacturing industry is undergoing a fundamental transformation driven by the fourth industrial revolution,
commonly referred to as Industry 4.0. This transformation is characterized by \ac{CPS},
intelligent automation, and the convergence of technologies including mechatronic systems,
controllers, on-board computation, big data, machine learning, and the \ac{IIoT} \cite{lasi2014industry}.

Industry 4.0 holds the promise of increased flexibility in manufacturing, along with mass customization, better quality, and improved productivity \cite{zhong2017intelligent}.
Its vision encompasses ecosystems of smart factories with intelligent and autonomous shop-floor entities \cite{vaidya2018industry}.
To provide a structured approach for implementing this vision, the Platform Industrie 4.0 working group developed the Reference Architectural Model Industrie 4.0 (\ac{RAMI4.0}) \cite{fraile2019reference}.

    \centering

    \caption{Reference Architectural Model Industrie 4.0 (RAMI 4.0) \cite{lindner2023digital}}
    \label{fig:RAMI4.0}
\end{figure} \\
\ac{RAMI4.0} is a three-dimensional map showing how to approach the deployment of Industry 4.0 in a structured manner, ensuring that all participants involved share a common perspective and develop a common understanding \cite{lindner2023digital}.
The model encompasses three axes: the product life cycle and value stream (distinguishing between types and instances),
the hierarchy levels representing different participants within the network,
and six interoperability layers covering everything from business processes to communication standards to physical components \cite{lindner2023digital}.

Central to this architectural model is the concept of the \ac{DT}.
A digital twin can be defined as an evolving digital profile of the historical
and current behavior of a physical object or process that helps
optimize business performance \cite{vanderhorn2021digital}.
Digital twins allow industry to detect physical issues sooner,
predict outcomes more accurately, and build better products \cite{vanderhorn2021digital}.
As the adoption of Industry 4.0 advances, digital twins prove invaluable for testing and simulating new parameters and design variants \cite{attaran2023impact}.

To fully realize the potential of digital twins in industrial settings,
standardization is essential \cite{inigo2020towards}.
The \ac{AAS} has emerged as a key enabler in this regard.
The \ac{AAS} is the logical representation of an asset that enables it to communicate in an Industry 4.0-compliant manner \cite{inigo2020towards}.
Together with the asset, the \ac{AAS} forms the Industry 4.0 Component (I4.0 Component) \cite{inigo2020towards}.

The \ac{AAS} is the standard digital representation of an industrial asset that plays a pivotal role in enabling interoperable communication among \ac{I4.0} components across the value chain \cite{inigo2020towards}.
The interoperability between I4.0 components depends crucially on the contents of the administration shell,
and so it is the main task of the \ac{AAS} to register and make available the data and functions of all relevant assets in a standardized way throughout their life cycle \cite{wei2019review}.

However, as manufacturing systems become increasingly complex, autonomous production becomes essential.
As \cite{wang2016implementing} note, the increasing complexity caused by global competition, product variety, 
and individualization demands production systems that are self-organized and capable of autonomous 
decision-making \cite{wang2016implementing}. In such environments, it becomes impossible to anticipate and 
automate all relevant constellations through centralized control \cite{antons2024designing}.

% ----- Problem statement -----

While the \ac{AAS} provides a robust framework for standardizing asset representation and communication,
current implementations predominantly focus on passive data exchange and reactive behavior \cite{leitao2025role}.
Most existing solutions are limited to passive (providing static metadata) or reactive (responding to external events) functionalities,
lacking the active behaviors required to support fully autonomous interactions and intelligent decision-making \cite{pereira2025aas}.
The \ac{Type3AAS}, which is characterized by its proactive capabilities, remains largely unexplored in practice,
still in its early stages and lacking formal specifications and comprehensive implementation guidelines \cite{leitao2025role}.
Realizing truly autonomous \ac{I4.0} components requires not only standardized data models but also 
decision-making capabilities that allow assets to act proactively based on their goals and beliefs.
This gap motivates the integration of agent-based technologies with the \ac{AAS} concept.
% ----- Brief conceptual grounding -----
\ac{MAS} offer a promising paradigm for implementing autonomous behavior in distributed systems.
In particular, the \ac{BDI} architecture provides a well-established model for rational agents 
that reason about their beliefs, desires, and intentions to select appropriate actions \cite{rao1995bdi}.
By combining the standardized structure of the \ac{AAS} with the proactive reasoning capabilities of \ac{BDI} agents,
it becomes possible to realize \ac{Type3AAS} that can autonomously manage and optimize asset operations.

% ----- Contribution of the thesis -----

This thesis proposes an approach to implementing \ac{Type3AAS} using \ac{MAS} based on the \ac{BDI} architecture.
The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A conceptual integration of \ac{AAS} with \ac{BDI}-based agents
    \item Foundational submodels for enabling proactive behavior in \ac{AAS}
    \item A proof-of-concept implementation demonstrated through a production resource use case involving drill machines
\end{itemize}
